# Promoter DNA Methylation Adds Little to Promoter Chromatin Accessibility in Explaining Gene Expression Differences Between Tumors: A Pre-Specified Test in Nine Cancer Types

## Simple Summary
DNA methylation at the start of a gene is often read as a sign that the gene is switched off in a tumor. We asked how well this mark explains why a gene is more active in some tumors than in others of the same cancer type, and whether it adds anything to how open, or accessible, the DNA is at the start of the gene. We used public data on tumors of ten cancer types. For a typical gene, methylation alone explained at most about 5% of the differences between tumors and added little or nothing once openness was known. Openness did better than methylation on average, but not in prostate or stomach cancer, where both did poorly. Methylation was informative for a recurring set of genes, including zinc-finger, germline and imprinted genes. Outside such genes, promoter methylation alone is weak evidence that a gene is silenced.

## Abstract
**Background/Objectives:** Promoter DNA methylation is widely read as marking gene silencing in tumors but is rarely compared with promoter chromatin accessibility in the same tumors. In 73 breast tumors, accessibility explained far more cross-tumor expression variance than methylation; we tested this in other cancer types. **Methods:** We analyzed primary tumors from The Cancer Genome Atlas with matched ATAC-seq, HumanMethylation450 and RNA-seq data, under an analysis plan hashed before any non-breast values were read. Per gene and cancer type, leave-one-out cross-validated R² (CV-R²) of models of expression on promoter methylation, accessibility or both was summarized by its median; types were combined by random-effects meta-analysis. **Results:** In nine test types (191 tumors), adding methylation to accessibility did not improve prediction (pooled median gain −0.046; 95% CI −0.060 to −0.032; co-primary criterion met), and in 266 tumors pooled within type the gain was 0.003 (−0.001 to 0.008). Relative to predicting each tumor by its type mean, methylation alone explained a median −4% to 5% of expression variance per type and 2.1% pooled, against 17.2% for accessibility. Accessibility outperformed methylation on average (pooled difference 0.130; 0.053 to 0.207) but in only seven of nine types (not prostate or stomach), missing the co-primary consistency criterion. The two layers were modestly coupled (pooled median Spearman ρ −0.27). Exploratory analyses found 59 genes, including zinc-finger, germline and imprinted genes, methylation-informative in at least five of ten cohorts. **Conclusions:** Across cancer types, promoter methylation carries little information about expression differences between tumors beyond promoter accessibility, except for a recurring set of genes.

**Keywords:** DNA methylation; chromatin accessibility; ATAC-seq; gene expression; pan-cancer; TCGA; multi-omics; meta-analysis; pre-specified analysis

## 1. Introduction

Promoter DNA methylation is the most widely measured epigenetic mark in cancer. It is stable, easy to assay on arrays and is often read as evidence that a gene is silenced [@weber2007; @esteller2024]. Integrations of methylation and expression data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) are still used to nominate methylation biomarkers [@mashayekh2026], and pan-cancer analyses report that tumor-specific promoter hypermethylation is accompanied by lower expression of the affected genes [@halaburkova2020]. Yet promoters can also be silenced without methylation, methylation can persist at promoters that remain active [@suzuki2008], and transcription-factor binding depends on both chromatin accessibility and methylation state [@klemm2019; @ochoa2023]. Removing methylation does not by itself open chromatin: after pharmacological demethylation of colorectal cancer cells, fewer than 2% of demethylated regions became accessible [@pandiyan2013].

Chromatin accessibility can now be measured in primary tumors. Corces et al. profiled accessibility by ATAC-seq (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing) in about 400 primary tumors of 23 cancer types from TCGA, most of which also have methylation and RNA-seq data [@corces2018]. Accessibility has since been used to filter methylation data, as supporting evidence at selected loci [@aine2025; @lehmann2021] or, in breast cancer, as a predictor of intrinsic subtype [@park2024]. Read-level analyses show that the pattern of methylation on single DNA molecules tracks newly accessible chromatin in tumors independently of average methylation levels [@zhang2025ijms], so array-based averages may miss part of the link between the two layers. How much each layer tells us about why a gene's expression differs between tumors of the same cancer type has not, to our knowledge, been measured across cancer types.

Such a comparison has an asymmetry that shapes what can be concluded. Accessibility at a transcription start site (TSS) is partly a consequence of transcription, so a strong association between promoter accessibility and expression is expected and does not show that accessibility drives expression. The question that the data can answer is how much information promoter methylation carries about expression, on its own and beyond accessibility. This matters for how methylation differences between tumors are interpreted, because they are often taken as evidence of silencing when expression or accessibility data are absent.

We first examined this question in 73 primary breast tumors with all three assays, under an analysis plan committed before outcomes were examined [@breast2026zenodo]. Promoter accessibility explained a median 19.6% of cross-tumor expression variance in leave-one-out cross-validation, methylation 0.5%, and adding methylation to accessibility did not improve prediction. Here we treat that result as a source of hypotheses and test it in the other TCGA cancer types with matched data, under a second plan committed before any non-breast methylation, accessibility or expression value was read. The plan fixed three criteria in advance; we report all three, including one that was not met.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Study Design and Analysis Plans
This is a secondary, observational analysis of public tumor data; no new samples were collected. It had two stages. The breast stage followed an internal analysis plan committed on 16 November 2026 (SHA-256 beginning b2b7079a); its code and results were archived before the second stage began [@breast2026zenodo]. The pan-cancer stage followed an internal analysis plan committed on 25 November 2026 at 00:03 UTC (SHA-256 beginning c440f092) and pushed at the same time to the public code repository, whose commit history records the time. At that point no methylation, accessibility or expression value of a non-breast tumor had been read; only sample identifiers, array platforms and file headers had been checked. Breast is therefore the discovery cohort and the nine other cancer types that met the plan's size criterion are the test. Full hashes are given in Supplementary Table S1, which also lists every change made after each commit and states whether it was written before or after the relevant results were seen.

The pan-cancer plan stated three hypotheses for tumors of the same cancer type: promoter accessibility explains more cross-tumor expression variance than promoter methylation (H-A); adding methylation to accessibility adds little (H-B); and the two layers are only weakly negatively correlated (H-C).

### 2.2. Data
*Chromatin accessibility:* cancer type-specific ATAC-seq peak sets and log2-normalized insertion counts [@corces2018], obtained from the NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC) publication page; technical replicates were averaged per tumor. ATAC-seq quality statistics (deduplicated reads, TSS enrichment, fraction of reads in peaks and duplicate fraction) came from the same study's Data S1. *DNA methylation:* GDC harmonized SeSAMe level-3 beta values from the Illumina HumanMethylation450 (HM450) array, one primary-tumor file per sample, with hg38 positions and CpG-island annotation from the Zhou lab manifests and probes flagged by the general mask of the same resource removed [@zhou2017]. *Gene expression:* RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization) gene-level values (log2(x + 1)) from the UCSC Xena TCGA hub [@goldman2020]. *Tumor purity:* ABSOLUTE estimates from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas [@carter2012]. Each downloaded file was checked against its published MD5 checksum, and SHA-256 checksums of all inputs are archived with the code.

### 2.3. Cohorts
The unit of analysis was one primary tumor per donor with all three assays, linked by the 15-character TCGA sample barcode. Of 404 tumors with ATAC-seq across 23 cancer types, 353 primary tumors had all three assays: 73 breast tumors (43 on HM450, 30 on the earlier HumanMethylation27 array) and 280 tumors of 21 other types. Glioblastoma had no RNA-seq for its ATAC-seq samples. Four non-breast tumors with HumanMethylation27 data only were excluded, leaving 276. The plan defined the test cohort as the non-breast types with at least 15 such tumors: kidney papillary (KIRP, 33), prostate (PRAD, 26), colon (COAD, 23), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD, 21), stomach (STAD, 21), esophagus (ESCA, 18), liver (LIHC, 17), lung squamous (LUSC, 16) and kidney clear cell carcinoma (KIRC, 16), a total of 191 tumors (Table 1). The 17 non-breast types with at least 5 tumors (266 tumors) entered a pooled within-type analysis; four types with fewer than 5 tumors were not analyzed.

### 2.4. Promoter Features
Definitions were fixed in the plan and are identical to the breast analysis unless stated. Genes were protein-coding GENCODE v36 basic genes, with the TSS at the most 5′ basic transcript and the promoter window from 1000 bp upstream to 500 bp downstream of the TSS. Promoter methylation was the mean beta value of the unmasked HM450 probes in the window, requiring at least two probes. In breast, only probes shared by both arrays were used, with array platform as a covariate. Promoter accessibility was the maximum normalized ATAC-seq signal among that cancer type's peaks overlapping the window. A promoter was a CpG-island promoter if a probe in the window lay in a CpG island. Genes were expressed if log2 RSEM ≥ 1 in at least 20% of tumors of the type. Within each type, genes with complete data were analyzed if variable in all three layers: methylation SD ≥ 0.05, accessibility SD above the type's median and expression SD ≥ 0.5.

### 2.5. Models and Per-Type Statistics
For each gene and type, three linear models of expression were fitted on z-scored promoter methylation (M), promoter accessibility (A) or both (MA). Performance was measured by leave-one-tumor-out cross-validation from the prediction error sum of squares (PRESS), computed in closed form. As specified in the plan, CV-R² was 1 − PRESS/SST, where SST is the sum of squares about the mean of all tumors of the type. On this scale, a model that predicts each held-out tumor by the mean of the other tumors scores 1 − (n/(n − 1))², from −0.063 for 33 tumors to −0.138 for 16, not zero. To say whether a model predicts better than the cancer-type mean, we therefore also report, post hoc, CV-R² relative to that leave-one-out mean model, 1 − PRESS/PRESS₀ (for breast, relative to a model with array platform only). This is the scale already used by the pooled within-type analysis (Section 2.7); on it, zero means no better than the type mean. Per-gene values are given in Supplementary Table S2.

For each type we computed D, the median over genes of CV-R²(A) − CV-R²(M); G, the median of CV-R²(MA) − CV-R²(A); the median CV-R² of each model; and the fraction of genes with CV-R²(A) > CV-R²(M). Confidence intervals (95%) came from a delete-one-tumor jackknife (estimate ± 1.96 SE) [@efron1982].

### 2.6. Pre-Specified Criteria
D and G were combined across the nine test types by DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis using jackknife standard errors [@dersimonian1986], with heterogeneity summarized by I² [@higgins2002]. The plan fixed one primary and two co-primary criteria, on the plan's CV-R² scale. R1 (primary, H-A): pooled D > 0 with a 95% CI excluding zero. R2 (co-primary consistency criterion, H-A): D > 0 in at least eight of the nine types (one-sided sign test, p = 0.020). R3 (co-primary, H-B): the upper 95% limit of pooled G below 0.02, an equivalence margin intended to mean that methylation adds less than 2 percentage points of explained variance. All three were to be reported whatever they showed.

### 2.7. Secondary Analyses
Pre-specified secondary analyses were: (S1, H-C) per-type median Spearman correlation between promoter methylation and accessibility across tumors, for genes with methylation SD ≥ 0.05 and a promoter peak, the fraction with ρ ≤ −0.3, and a random-effects pooled median ρ; (S2) all non-breast tumors of the 17 types with at least 5 tumors pooled, with cancer-type indicators in every model, predictors z-scored within type and CV-R² measured against the type-only model; (S3) CpG-island and non-island promoters separately; (S4) ABSOLUTE purity added to every model; (S5) breast re-analyzed with the pan-cancer code and a meta-analysis including breast; (S6) heterogeneity; and (S7) the primary analysis repeated with the breast probe set.

### 2.8. Negative Controls
Two negative controls were run in each test type. First, a technical-only model predicted each gene's expression from ATAC-seq deduplicated reads (log10), TSS enrichment, fraction of reads in peaks, duplicate fraction and sample-mean methylation, with the same cross-validation. Second, in a mismatched-promoter null, each gene was given the methylation and accessibility of a random gene from the same expression-mean decile (100 draws).

### 2.9. Post Hoc Analyses
After the primary results were seen, we added analyses that were not in either plan and report them as exploratory (Supplementary Table S1, entries 3–5). The emphasis of this paper, led by the co-primary criterion for H-B and by the pooled within-type analysis rather than by H-A as in the plan's working title, was also chosen after the results were seen. In response to an internal review, entries 3 and 4 added: (a) the gene selection repeated within every jackknife replicate, so that the intervals include selection uncertainty; (b) strata by the number of probes in the promoter window (2–3, 4–7, ≥ 8); (c) genes with methylation-only CV-R² > 0.3 in each cohort and their recurrence across the ten cohorts, compared with 1000 draws in which each cohort's number of informative genes was drawn at random from the genes it tested; (d) a two-state methylation model in which each gene's tumors were split at the threshold maximizing the between-group variance of beta values [@otsu1979], with at least 3 tumors per group; (e) mean ATAC-seq TSS enrichment against D across types; and (f) a single-predictor version of the mismatched-promoter null. After a second internal pre-submission review, entry 5 added: (g) the type-mean-relative CV-R² described in Section 2.5; (h) the random-effects pooled median ρ specified for S1, which the analysis code had replaced with a tumor-weighted mean; (i) Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman confidence intervals and 95% prediction intervals for pooled D and G [@inthout2014]; (j) the recurrence analysis (c) repeated for accessibility-informative genes, as a comparator; and (k) the fraction of genes for which methylation outperformed accessibility by more than 0.1.

### 2.10. Software and Reproducibility
Analyses used Python 3.13.18 with NumPy 2.5.3, pandas 3.0.5, SciPy 1.18.1, Matplotlib 3.11.2 and openpyxl 3.1.5. Random seeds were fixed, and a repeat run of the primary analysis from the processed inputs reproduced every output file exactly. Code, both analysis plans with their hashes, the deviations logs, all outputs and input checksums are available at https://github.com/MohannadBanat/brca-promoter-methylation-accessibility (archived version {{VERSION}}: https://doi.org/{{DOI}}).

*Use of generative artificial intelligence.* Claude (Anthropic; models Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5) was used to help design both analysis plans, to write and run the analysis code, to search the literature, to prepare the figures, to draft the manuscript and to carry out the internal pre-submission reviews mentioned in Section 2.9. Both plans were fixed and hashed before the outcomes they cover were computed, and all later changes are recorded with their timing (Supplementary Table S1). The authors reviewed the analyses and the manuscript and take full responsibility for them.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Cohorts
The nine test types contributed 191 tumors and between 1536 and 3880 genes each that were variable in all three layers, 22,490 gene–type pairs in total (Table 1). With the 73 breast tumors and the 75 tumors of eight smaller types that entered only the pooled analysis, 339 tumors were analyzed. The breast cohort contributed 1193 genes. Re-running breast through the pan-cancer code reproduced the breast-stage values exactly, with or without the explicit probe mask; detailed breast results are given in Supplementary Table S3.

### 3.2. Methylation Alone Explained Little of the Variation in Expression Between Tumors
On the plan's scale, the median CV-R² of the methylation-only model ranged from −0.188 in LUSC to −0.036 in PRAD (Table 2). Relative to the leave-one-out type-mean model (Table 3; Figure 1), methylation alone explained a median of −4.4% (LUSC) to 4.9% (STAD) of expression variance, better than the type mean in four types (KIRP, PRAD, STAD and ESCA) and worse in five. No upper 95% limit exceeded 0.114. In breast, the corresponding value was 3.6% (1.9% to 5.4%), and with all 266 non-breast tumors pooled within type it was 2.1% (1.2% to 3.1%). Accessibility explained −0.5% (PRAD) to 25.9% (KIRC) per type and 17.2% (14.7% to 19.8%) in the pooled analysis.

Methylation nonetheless carried some information. In an exploratory comparison, an unrelated gene's methylation from the same expression decile gave a median CV-R² between −0.245 (LUSC) and −0.096 (KIRP) on the plan's scale, and observed methylation exceeded that baseline by 0.039 to 0.095 (Supplementary Table S4). Accessibility exceeded the same baseline by 0.051 in PRAD to 0.390 in KIRC.

### 3.3. Methylation Added Little or Nothing to Accessibility
Pooled G was −0.046 (95% CI −0.060 to −0.032; I² 82%), so the co-primary criterion R3 was met (Figure 2B; Table 2). In seven types, G was below zero with a CI excluding zero. In PRAD (0.015; −0.036 to 0.066) and STAD (0.001; −0.052 to 0.055), G was close to zero with upper limits above 0.02, so equivalence was not shown in those two types. R3 is a lenient test at these sample sizes, because G includes the cross-validation cost of an extra predictor, which is larger in small samples. The more informative estimate is therefore the pooled within-type analysis of 266 tumors, in which that cost is small: G was 0.003 (−0.001 to 0.008). In the 73 breast tumors it was −0.004 (−0.011 to 0.002), and including breast in the meta-analysis gave a pooled G of −0.040 (−0.058 to −0.021).

### 3.4. Accessibility Outperformed Methylation on Average but Not in Every Type
Pooled D was 0.130 (95% CI 0.053 to 0.207), so R1 was met (Figure 2A). Heterogeneity was high (I² 81%; 95% prediction interval −0.135 to 0.395), and D was positive in seven of the nine types (one-sided sign test p = 0.090), so the co-primary criterion R2 (eight of nine) was not met. The exceptions were PRAD (D −0.048; −0.110 to 0.014) and STAD (−0.021; −0.148 to 0.105). In both, the two layers were similarly weak: relative to the type mean, accessibility explained −0.5% and methylation 4.2% in PRAD, and 4.8% and 4.9% in STAD. In an exploratory count, methylation outperformed accessibility by more than 0.1 for 41% of PRAD genes and 37% of STAD genes, against 11–21% in the other test types. In LIHC and LUSC, D was positive but accessibility was also weak (11.8% and 10.1%). Including breast, D was 0.131 (0.066 to 0.196) and positive in eight of ten cohorts. In the pooled within-type analysis, D was 0.123 (0.100 to 0.147), and accessibility outperformed methylation for 95.6% of genes.

Across the nine types, D was not clearly associated with the number of tumors, mean purity or, in an exploratory comparison, mean ATAC-seq TSS enrichment (Spearman ρ −0.32, −0.27 and 0.27; Supplementary Tables S4 and S5).

### 3.5. Methylation and Accessibility Were Modestly Coupled
Among genes with variable promoter methylation and a promoter peak, the median per-gene Spearman correlation between methylation and accessibility across tumors ranged from −0.16 (STAD) to −0.36 (ESCA) (Table 1), against −0.19 in breast. The pooled median ρ was −0.268 (95% CI −0.299 to −0.237; I² 1%). The fraction of genes with ρ ≤ −0.3 ranged from 31.7% to 57.9%. Coupling was therefore consistently negative but modest: for a large share of genes, differences in promoter accessibility between tumors were not matched by differences in methylation.

### 3.6. Robustness
The pooled results held at CpG-island and non-island promoters, after adjustment for purity and with the breast probe set (Table 4). In every test type, the combined model exceeded the technical-only model for 81–90% of genes, and its median CV-R² was above the 95% range of the mismatched-promoter null (Supplementary Table S5).

The post hoc checks did not change the pooled results (Table 4; Supplementary Table S4). On the type-mean-relative scale, pooled D was 0.117 (0.048 to 0.186) and pooled G −0.041 (−0.054 to −0.029). Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman intervals were 0.051 to 0.209 for D and −0.066 to −0.026 for G. With gene selection repeated within each jackknife replicate, pooled D was 0.131 (0.055 to 0.207) and pooled G −0.045 (−0.060 to −0.031). The median methylation-only CV-R² was below zero on the plan's scale in all 27 combinations of type and probe-number stratum, including promoters with at least eight probes, so the weak performance of methylation does not reflect a shortage of probes. A two-state model, which treats methylation as present or absent, did worse than the linear model in every type: it outperformed the linear model for 36–49% of genes, and adding it to accessibility lowered the median CV-R² in every type.

### 3.7. Exploratory: Genes Where Methylation Is Informative
Medians hide genes for which methylation is informative. In each cohort, 7.6% (LIHC) to 18.0% (PRAD) of genes had a methylation-only CV-R² above 0.3 (Supplementary Table S6). These genes recurred across cohorts (Figure 3A): 59 were informative in at least five of the ten cohorts, against a mean of 1.1 (maximum 6) when the same numbers of genes were drawn at random. This comparison treats genes as interchangeable, and it is not specific to methylation: accessibility-informative genes also recurred more than at random (131 genes against a mean of 36.0), although by a smaller factor. Of the 59 genes, 17 encode zinc-finger proteins, 12 of them on chromosome 19, where the largest cluster of Krüppel-associated box zinc-finger genes lies [@sobocinska2021]. Others are germline or cancer-testis genes (*DDX43*, *LDHC*, *OOEP*, *SPESP1*), a class whose expression in tumors is linked to loss of promoter methylation [@fratta2011]; imprinted genes (*MKRN3*, *FAM50B*); and genes expressed in leukocytes (*PTPRCAP*, *HCLS1*, *MYO1G*, *ARHGAP30*) (Figure 3B). The gene informative in most cohorts, *BST2* (nine of ten), encodes an interferon-inducible protein and lies on chromosome 19 outside the zinc-finger cluster.

## 4. Discussion

Across nine cancer types tested under a pre-specified plan, and in the breast cohort that generated the hypotheses, promoter methylation added little or nothing to the prediction of gene expression once promoter accessibility was known. This held for the pooled estimate and in seven of the nine types, and it was clearest in the pooled within-type analysis, where the gain was 0.3 percentage points. On its own, methylation explained between −4% and 5% of expression variance at the median gene per type, relative to predicting each tumor by its type mean, and about 2% when many tumors were pooled. It carried more information than an unrelated gene's methylation, but little next to the 17% explained by accessibility in the same analysis. The two layers were consistently but modestly coupled. These results held at CpG-island and non-island promoters, after adjustment for purity and in post hoc checks of gene selection, probe number, the scale of CV-R², the meta-analytic method and a two-state model of methylation.

The comparison between the two layers did not replicate as cleanly. Accessibility outperformed methylation on average, but not in prostate and stomach cancer, where both layers were weak. We cannot explain these exceptions with the available data. Tumor number, purity and ATAC-seq quality were not clearly associated with D, and with nine types and many plausible causes, any explanation we offered would be post hoc. The failed consistency criterion and the wide prediction interval mean that this paper cannot claim that accessibility outperforms methylation in all cancers. Its claim is narrower: methylation adds little to accessibility, on the pooled estimate and in most cancers tested.

The asymmetry noted in the Introduction limits what this means. Accessibility at a TSS partly reflects transcription, so the strong performance of accessibility is not evidence that it controls expression. Nor does the small gain from adding methylation show that methylation is irrelevant. If methylation silenced a gene by closing its promoter, its information would be contained in accessibility, and G would be close to zero. The modest coupling argues against this being the usual case: across tumors, much of the variation in promoter accessibility was not mirrored by methylation. This is consistent with methylation being one of several routes to an inactive promoter, alongside Polycomb repression and histone modification [@suzuki2008; @chenprc2020; @noberini2025], and with the observation that demethylation rarely opens chromatin [@pandiyan2013]. The conclusion that is robust to the asymmetry concerns methylation alone. For a typical variable gene, a difference in promoter methylation between tumors of the same type is weak evidence of a difference in expression.

The exploratory analysis suggests where methylation does matter. The genes for which methylation was informative recurred across cancer types more than genes drawn at random, and several belong to classes known to be switched on or off by methylation. Cancer-testis genes are silenced by promoter methylation in somatic tissue, and their expression in tumors is linked to its loss [@fratta2011]. Imprinted genes carry methylation that marks the silenced parental allele. Many promoters of the Krüppel-associated box zinc-finger genes on chromosome 19 are methylated in cancers [@sobocinska2021]. The leukocyte genes probably reflect a different mechanism: their methylation marks the immune-cell content of the sample rather than regulation within tumor cells [@zheng2017]. These classes account for fewer than half of the 59 recurrent genes, and the random-gene comparison ignores differences between genes. The hypothesis is therefore tentative: promoter methylation may be a good readout of expression for genes under binary, methylation-dependent control and a poor one for most other variable genes. A test in independent data could confirm or refute this.

These findings bear on how promoter methylation is used. A methylation difference at a promoter, measured on an array and taken alone, is weak evidence that the gene is silenced in a tumor, except possibly for genes in classes like those above. Where accessibility or expression can be measured, it is the more direct readout. Methylation-based biomarkers and subtypes need not be invalid, since they may track tumor or cell identity rather than the expression of the gene they sit on. But they should not be interpreted gene by gene as evidence of silencing without supporting data.

The study has limitations. It is observational, and all three assays are bulk measurements on different aliquots of each tumor, averaged over tumor, stromal and immune cells. The test types are small (16–33 tumors), which makes per-type estimates imprecise; the jackknife intervals use a normal approximation, and with nine types the between-type variance is poorly estimated, although Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman intervals gave the same conclusions. The equivalence criterion R3 is lenient at these sample sizes, as explained in Section 3.3. The HM450 array measures few CpGs per promoter, and averaging beta values discards read-level information that tracks accessibility [@zhang2025ijms]. Sequencing-based or single-molecule methods that read methylation and accessibility together [@clark2018; @pott2017; @barnett2020] could test whether the modest coupling reflects the assays or the biology. We analyzed promoters only and used linear models. The post hoc analyses, including the type-mean-relative scale and the emphasis of the paper, were not pre-specified. Finally, the ATAC-seq data cover a few hundred TCGA tumors, and no independent cohort with all three assays in several cancer types was available to us.

## 5. Conclusions

In nine cancer types tested under a pre-specified plan, and in a breast discovery cohort, promoter DNA methylation added little or nothing to promoter chromatin accessibility in explaining why a gene's expression differs between tumors of the same type. On its own, it explained at most about 5% of the variation for a typical gene. Accessibility outperformed methylation on average, but not in every type. Methylation was informative for a recurring set of genes, including zinc-finger, germline and imprinted genes. Outside such genes, a promoter methylation difference should not be read as evidence of silencing without supporting expression or accessibility data.
